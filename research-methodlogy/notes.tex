\chapter{Introduction}

Understanding what \textit{research} actually means is the first step in building a solid mental model for any project. If you don't get the definition right, you end up just "data-dredging" instead of creating something of value
.
Think of this as the "OS" of our academic and professional work. Let’s break it down into a system you can use.

\begin{layerELI5}
Imagine you have a really tricky puzzle, but some of the pieces are hidden around the house. Research isn't just picking up the pieces you find by accident; it’s making a plan to look under every rug and in every drawer until you can put the whole picture together and show your friends how you found it.
\end{layerELI5}


\begin{layerReal}
	\textbf{What:} Research is a scientific and systematic search for pertinent information on a specific topic
	. In a technical sense, it involves defining and redefining problems, formulating hypotheses, and collecting, organising, and evaluating data to reach conclusions
	.
	
	
	\textbf{Why:} The core purpose is to find out things in a systematic way to increase knowledge
	. It aims to discover the truth that is hidden or hasn't been discovered yet
	. More practically, we do research whenever we gather information to answer a question that solves a problem
	.
	
	
	\textbf{How:} It functions as a movement from the known to the unknown—a "voyage of discovery"
	. This requires a combination of theory and methodology tailored to a specific problem
	. It must be "\textit{systematic}," meaning it is based on logical relationships rather than just beliefs
	.
	
	
	\textbf{When/Where:} It is used whenever a researcher experiences a difficulty in either a theoretical or practical situation and wants a solution
	. It happens in labs, libraries, the "field," and even in our own homes
	.
\end{layerReal}

\begin{layerTakeaways}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Originality:} Research must be an original contribution to the existing stock of knowledge
		.
		
		
		\item \textbf{Systematic Logic:} It isn't just gathering facts; it’s the systematic interpretation of data with a clear purpose
		.
		
		
		\item \textbf{The Social Contract:} Research is a profoundly social activity that connects you to those who will use your findings to improve their lives or understanding
		.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{layerTakeaways}

\begin{misconception}
	\textbf{Misconception:} Research is just \textit{intelligence gathering} or \textit{fact-finding}
	.
	
	
	\textbf{Reality:} Simply reassembling or reordering facts without interpretation is NOT research
	. True research requires you to \textit{scratch a mental itch} by answering a \textit{how} or \textit{why} question that moves beyond mere description
	.
\end{misconception}

\begin{optionalExplore}
	\textbf{Deductive vs. Inductive Approaches:} Testing existing theories vs. building new ones from data
	.
	
	
	\textbf{The Research Pyramid:} Understanding the levels of paradigms, methodology, methods, and techniques
	.
\end{optionalExplore}

\section{Objective of Research}

4
In the introduction phase, defining your \textbf{research objectives} is the process of turning a general "itch" into a specific, executable mission. Think of objectives as the bridge between your curiosity and your results.

\begin{layerELI5}
    Imagine you're a detective trying to solve a mystery. You don't just walk around looking at everything. You have a goal: "Find out who took the cookie, how they got into the kitchen, and why they didn't take the milk." Those specific goals are your \textbf{objectives}. They tell you exactly what you need to find so you don't waste time looking at things that don't matter, like the color of the floor or what's on TV.
\end{layerELI5}

\begin{layerReal}
    \textbf{What:} A research objective is a specific, clear, and "to the point" statement of what you intend to accomplish. It defines the intended outcomes—whether that's new knowledge, a model, or a set of suggestions for improvement.
    
    
    \textbf{Why:} The primary purpose of research is to discover truths that are currently hidden. Objectives provide a "road map" or "blueprint" for the study. Without them, you cannot determine what data is relevant, what techniques to use, or how to write the final report.
    
    
    \textbf{How:} Objectives are typically derived from your \textbf{Research Question}. While the question asks "How?" or "Why?", the objective uses formal language and action verbs (like \textit{identify, determine, describe}) to specify the intended outcome. In business research, objectives are often expected to pass the \textbf{SMART} test: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, and Timely.
    
    
    \textbf{When/Where:} Objectives must be defined at the very beginning of the research process, during the design phase. They are the starting point for both \textbf{Fundamental Research} (seeking knowledge for its own sake) and \textbf{Applied Research} (seeking a solution to a practical problem).
    
    
    \textbf{Who:} The objectives serve to synchronise three key stakeholders: the researcher (you), the client (the person with the problem), and the scientific community (your supervisors/examiners).
\end{layerReal}

\begin{layerTakeaways}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Truth Discovery:} The ultimate aim of any objective is to find a truth that has not yet been discovered.
        \item \textbf{The Four-Way Taxonomy:} Most objectives fall into one of four buckets: \textbf{Exploratory} (familiarity), \textbf{Descriptive} (portraying characteristics), \textbf{Diagnostic} (frequency/association), or \textbf{Hypothesis-Testing} (causal links).
        \item \textbf{Action Over Aim:} Objectives are more than just "aims." Aims are general intents, whereas objectives are specific, observable outcomes.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{layerTakeaways}

\begin{misconception}
    \textbf{The "Word-Spinning" Trap:} Many researchers think that a long, fancy-sounding introduction is the same as having clear objectives. It isn't. The sources warn that research significance lies in \textbf{quality, not quantity}. If you can't state your objective in a line or two, you haven't defined your problem well enough yet.
\end{misconception}

\section{Research Motivation}

we often talk about the "why" as the fuel for the "how." Research is a marathon, and without a clear understanding of what's driving you, it's easy to hit a wall when the data gets messy or the literature review feels endless.

Let's architect our understanding of research motivation so you can build a system that keeps you moving.

\begin{layerELI5}
    Imagine you are a detective. Why would you want to solve a mystery? Maybe because you love puzzles, maybe because you want to help people, or maybe because it's your job and you want to get promoted. Motivation is simply the reason you keep looking for clues even when you are tired.
\end{layerELI5}

\begin{layerReal}
    \textbf{What:} Motivation in research is the set of internal and external factors that compel an investigator to undertake a systematic search for knowledge. 
    
    
    \textbf{Why:} It solves the problem of "effort sustainability." High-quality research requires significant time (often 6 months to several years) and intellectual energy; without strong motivation, the quality of the work suffers.
    
    
    \textbf{How:} Motivation functions through several channels:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Intellectual Curiosity:} The "mental itch" to scratch a specific question.
        \item \textbf{Puzzle Solving:} The "sheer pleasure" of discovering something no one else knows.
        \item \textbf{Extrinsic Rewards:} Earning a PhD, career advancement, or fulfilling a government directive.
        \item \textbf{Societal Impact:} A desire to be of service or solve pressing practical problems.
    \end{itemize}
    
    
    \textbf{When/Where:} Motivation is most critical during the \textbf{Introduction and Topic Selection} phase (to ensure the topic is interesting enough to finish) and the \textbf{Data Collection} phase (where the work becomes "hard" and less "routinized").
\end{layerReal}

\begin{layerTakeaways}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{The Interest-Quality Link:} If you are bored with your topic, your research quality will likely fail.
        \item \textbf{Gusto as a Metric:} Treating research as a "treasure hunt" or a "game" increases the likelihood of success.
        \item \textbf{The Double Context:} In management research, you often balance personal excitement with the pragmatic needs of an organization.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{layerTakeaways}

\begin{misconception}
    \textbf{Misconception:} You should only choose a topic you are "passionate" about.
    
    
    \textbf{Reality:} While interest is vital, "passion" must be balanced with \textbf{feasibility}. You might be passionate about a topic but lack access to data, subjects, or equipment, which leads to a "dead end".
\end{misconception}

\section{Types of Research}

In our circle, we don't just see these as academic labels; they are the "logic gates" of your research system. Depending on the type you choose, your entire workflow—from how you collect data to how you justify your conclusions—will shift fundamentally.

Let's break down these classifications so you can build a research design that actually fits the problem you're trying to solve.

\begin{layerELI5}
    Imagine you're in a big forest. 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Exploratory research} is like walking through for the first time just to see what kind of trees are there. 
        \item \textbf{Descriptive research} is like drawing a very detailed map of where every single tree is. 
        \item \textbf{Explanatory research} is trying to figure out why the trees on the north side are taller than the ones on the south side. 
        \item \textbf{Applied research} is finding the best tree to build a house with because you need a place to stay right now.
    \end{itemize}
\end{layerELI5}

\begin{layerReal}
    \textbf{What:} Research types are classifications based on the study's purpose (what you want to achieve), the nature of the data (numbers vs. words), and the setting (lab vs. field).
    
    
    \textbf{Why:} The primary purpose of research is to discover truths that are hidden. Identifying the "type" solves the problem of methodology mismatch; you wouldn't use a rigid experimental design to explore a fuzzy, brand-new social phenomenon.
    
    
    \textbf{How:} Research is categorized through several "binary" or continuous lenses:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Fundamental (Basic) vs. Applied:} Fundamental research seeks "knowledge for knowledge's sake" and theory formulation. Applied research aims to find an immediate solution for a practical problem facing society or an organization.
        \item \textbf{Descriptive vs. Analytical:} Descriptive research describes the "state of affairs" as it exists, often called \textit{Ex post facto} because the researcher cannot control the variables. Analytical research uses existing information to make a critical evaluation.
        \item \textbf{Quantitative vs. Qualitative:} Quantitative is based on the measurement of quantity or amount. Qualitative is concerned with "kind" or quality, often exploring the underlying motives and desires of human behavior.
        \item \textbf{Conceptual vs. Empirical:} Conceptual research relates to abstract ideas or theories (common for philosophers). Empirical research is data-based, relying on experience or observation that can be verified by experiment.
    \end{itemize}
    
    
    \textbf{When/Where:} These types are chosen during the \textbf{Research Design} phase. They apply in diverse settings: from the "pure" environment of a laboratory to the "fuzzy" reality of an organization.
\end{layerReal}

\begin{layerTakeaways}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{The Purpose-Process Link:} The type of research you choose dictates whether your design must be \textbf{flexible} (for discovery) or \textbf{rigid} (for testing).
        \item \textbf{The Continuum:} Research often sits on a continuum; for example, management research usually balances the "double hurdle" of academic rigour (fundamental) and practical relevance (applied).
        \item \textbf{Actionability:} "Applied" research is the "world of design," where scientific methods are used to solve concrete social or business problems.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{layerTakeaways}

\begin{misconception}
    \textbf{Misconception:} You have to pick only one type and stick to it perfectly.
    
    
    \textbf{Reality:} In practice, research styles often overlap. A study can be descriptive at the start (mapping the situation) and explanatory by the end (testing why it happened). Case studies, for instance, can combine both quantitative and qualitative techniques.
\end{misconception}

\section{Research Approach}

In the introductory phase of any study, choosing your approach isn't just about picking a "vibe"; it's about selecting the logic gate that determines how you will move from a fuzzy problem to a justifiable conclusion.

Think of this as the "master strategy" that bridges your philosophical worldview with the practical tools you'll use to gather evidence.

\begin{layerELI5}
    Imagine you want to learn about a new playground. You could bring a ruler and a stopwatch to measure how tall the slides are and how fast kids go down them—that's one approach. Or, you could sit on the bench and listen to the kids tell stories about which games they like most and why—that's another approach. You could even do both to get the full picture. The "approach" is just your decision on whether to use measurements, stories, or a mix of both to understand the playground.
\end{layerELI5}

\begin{layerReal}
    \textbf{What:} A research approach is a plan and procedure that spans decisions from broad assumptions to detailed methods of data collection and analysis. It is traditionally classified into three types: \textbf{qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods}. Rather than being polar opposites, these approaches exist on a \textbf{continuum}, where a study tends to be more qualitative than quantitative, or vice versa.
    
    
    \textbf{Why:} The primary purpose of selecting an approach is to ensure the research is \textbf{systematic} and based on logical relationships rather than mere beliefs. It solves the problem of "methodological mismatch"—for instance, you wouldn't use a rigid, numbered survey (quantitative) to understand the deeply personal, storied experiences of a single individual (qualitative).
    
    
    \textbf{How:} The approach is formed at the intersection of three components: \textbf{philosophical worldviews} (beliefs about reality), \textbf{strategies of inquiry} (methodological designs like ethnography or experiments), and \textbf{specific research methods} (data collection and analysis techniques). 
    
    
    \textbf{When/Where:} This decision is made during the \textbf{research design} phase. It is applied in any setting—from controlled laboratories for experiments to natural "field" settings like organisations where researchers observe behavior as it happens.
    
    
    \textbf{Who:} The researcher serves as the \textbf{key instrument}, responsible for aligning the chosen approach with the expectations of the \textbf{audience} (advisors, journal editors, or stakeholders).
    
    
    \textbf{Trade-offs:} 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Quantitative:} Offers high \textbf{reliability} and generalisability through statistical procedures but may "detach" the findings from the real-world context of the participants.
        \item \textbf{Qualitative:} Provides "thick" descriptions and deep \textbf{meaning} from the participant's perspective but can be criticized for its subjectivity and low repeatability.
    \end{itemize}
\end{layerReal}

\begin{layerTakeaways}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{The Continuum:} Research is rarely "purely" one type; it's a matter of emphasis on a scale between words (qualitative) and numbers (quantitative).
        \item \textbf{Logic of Inquiry:} Quantitative approaches typically follow a \textbf{deductive} logic (testing theories), while qualitative approaches follow an \textbf{inductive} logic (building theories from data).
        \item \textbf{The "Third Way":} Mixed methods research is an approach that combines both to provide a stronger understanding of a research problem than either alone.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{layerTakeaways}

\begin{misconception}
    \textbf{Misconception:} You must choose between qualitative and quantitative because they are incompatible.
    
    
    \textbf{Reality:} They are \textbf{complementary}. You can use qualitative research as a source of "hunches" or hypotheses that you then test quantitatively. Crotty argues the "great divide" between them actually happens at the level of \textit{methods} (tools), not necessarily at the level of philosophy.
\end{misconception}
