\chapter{Introduction}

Understanding what \textit{research} actually means is the first step in building a solid mental model for any project. If you don't get the definition right, you end up just "data-dredging" instead of creating something of value
.
Think of this as the "OS" of our academic and professional work. Let’s break it down into a system you can use.

\begin{layerELI5}
Imagine you have a really tricky puzzle, but some of the pieces are hidden around the house. Research isn't just picking up the pieces you find by accident; it’s making a plan to look under every rug and in every drawer until you can put the whole picture together and show your friends how you found it.
\end{layerELI5}


\begin{layerReal}
	\textbf{What:} Research is a scientific and systematic search for pertinent information on a specific topic
	. In a technical sense, it involves defining and redefining problems, formulating hypotheses, and collecting, organising, and evaluating data to reach conclusions
	.
	
	
	\textbf{Why:} The core purpose is to find out things in a systematic way to increase knowledge
	. It aims to discover the truth that is hidden or hasn't been discovered yet
	. More practically, we do research whenever we gather information to answer a question that solves a problem
	.
	
	
	\textbf{How:} It functions as a movement from the known to the unknown—a "voyage of discovery"
	. This requires a combination of theory and methodology tailored to a specific problem
	. It must be "\textit{systematic}," meaning it is based on logical relationships rather than just beliefs
	.
	
	
	\textbf{When/Where:} It is used whenever a researcher experiences a difficulty in either a theoretical or practical situation and wants a solution
	. It happens in labs, libraries, the "field," and even in our own homes
	.
\end{layerReal}

\begin{layerTakeaways}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Originality:} Research must be an original contribution to the existing stock of knowledge
		.
		
		
		\item \textbf{Systematic Logic:} It isn't just gathering facts; it’s the systematic interpretation of data with a clear purpose
		.
		
		
		\item \textbf{The Social Contract:} Research is a profoundly social activity that connects you to those who will use your findings to improve their lives or understanding
		.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{layerTakeaways}

\begin{misconception}
	\textbf{Misconception:} Research is just \textit{intelligence gathering} or \textit{fact-finding}
	.
	
	
	\textbf{Reality:} Simply reassembling or reordering facts without interpretation is NOT research
	. True research requires you to \textit{scratch a mental itch} by answering a \textit{how} or \textit{why} question that moves beyond mere description
	.
\end{misconception}

\begin{optionalExplore}
	\textbf{Deductive vs. Inductive Approaches:} Testing existing theories vs. building new ones from data
	.
	
	
	\textbf{The Research Pyramid:} Understanding the levels of paradigms, methodology, methods, and techniques
	.
\end{optionalExplore}

. You look at what everyone else has already said (the literature) so you don't \textit{reinvent the wheel}

\section{Objective of Research}


% ============================================================
%  Topic 2: RESEARCH OBJECTIVES
% ============================================================

In the introduction phase, defining your \textbf{research objectives} is the process of turning a general "itch" into a specific, executable mission. Think of objectives as the bridge between your curiosity and your results.

\begin{layerELI5}
    Imagine you're a detective trying to solve a mystery. You don't just walk around looking at everything. You have a goal: "Find out who took the cookie, how they got into the kitchen, and why they didn't take the milk." Those specific goals are your \textbf{objectives}. They tell you exactly what you need to find so you don't waste time looking at things that don't matter, like the color of the floor or what's on TV.
\end{layerELI5}

\begin{layerReal}
    \textbf{What:} A research objective is a specific, clear, and "to the point" statement of what you intend to accomplish. It defines the intended outcomes—whether that's new knowledge, a model, or a set of suggestions for improvement.
    
    
    \textbf{Why:} The primary purpose of research is to discover truths that are currently hidden. Objectives provide a "road map" or "blueprint" for the study. Without them, you cannot determine what data is relevant, what techniques to use, or how to write the final report.
    
    
    \textbf{How:} Objectives are typically derived from your \textbf{Research Question}. While the question asks "How?" or "Why?", the objective uses formal language and action verbs (like \textit{identify, determine, describe}) to specify the intended outcome. In business research, objectives are often expected to pass the \textbf{SMART} test: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, and Timely.
    
    
    \textbf{When/Where:} Objectives must be defined at the very beginning of the research process, during the design phase. They are the starting point for both \textbf{Fundamental Research} (seeking knowledge for its own sake) and \textbf{Applied Research} (seeking a solution to a practical problem).
    
    
    \textbf{Who:} The objectives serve to synchronise three key stakeholders: the researcher (you), the client (the person with the problem), and the scientific community (your supervisors/examiners).
\end{layerReal}

\begin{layerTakeaways}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Truth Discovery:} The ultimate aim of any objective is to find a truth that has not yet been discovered.
        \item \textbf{The Four-Way Taxonomy:} Most objectives fall into one of four buckets: \textbf{Exploratory} (familiarity), \textbf{Descriptive} (portraying characteristics), \textbf{Diagnostic} (frequency/association), or \textbf{Hypothesis-Testing} (causal links).
        \item \textbf{Action Over Aim:} Objectives are more than just "aims." Aims are general intents, whereas objectives are specific, observable outcomes.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{layerTakeaways}

\begin{misconception}
    \textbf{The "Word-Spinning" Trap:} Many researchers think that a long, fancy-sounding introduction is the same as having clear objectives. It isn't. The sources warn that research significance lies in \textbf{quality, not quantity}. If you can't state your objective in a line or two, you haven't defined your problem well enough yet.
\end{misconception}

        if intent.lower() not in action_verbs:
